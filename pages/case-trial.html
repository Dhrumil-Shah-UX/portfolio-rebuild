02 / Problem

Finding a trial is more than finding a medical match.

Patients must interpret unfamiliar language, weigh travel and visit requirements, and involve others without losing control of their information.

Comprehension

Medical details need context.

Terminology and fragmented trial information made clarity a core design concern.

Practical fit

Eligibility is only part of the decision.

Distance and appointment frequency also shape whether an option is practical.

Patient agency

Support needs boundaries.

Caregiver participation and peer communication require patient control.

Evidence foundation: literature review, affinity mapping, recurring physician collaboration, and three rounds of physician think-aloud testing. Testing involved physicians; real patients were not tested.

03 / Decisions

Clinical feedback, made visible in the interface.

Four decisions connect the research, prototype iterations, and the responsibilities I held.

Decision 01 · Discovery & clinical clarity

Suggest possibilities without implying eligibility.

What we learned

Physician feedback showed that “Matching” implied more certainty than the concept could support. Round 2 included terminology and data-sharing concerns; round 3 returned to suggestion accuracy.

What changed

The team renamed “Matching Trials” to “Suggested Trials” and kept eligibility considerations and clinician review visible in trial details.

Earlier labelMatching TrialsRevised labelSuggested Trials
Inspect trial details and clinical scope

A clinician dashboard and a broader physician/EHR decision hub were explored but not adopted because of their supporting feature requirements and scope. Selected elements informed the final concept.

Decision 02 · Onboarding & practical fit

Offer a shorter path without making record sharing compulsory.

What we learned

Earlier concepts used detailed manual history and document upload. Onboarding depth surfaced in rounds 1 and 3 of physician testing.

My response

I owned key onboarding flows and translated physician feedback into refinements. The team explored EHR sync while retaining manual entry for people who preferred not to share records.

See travel preferences and the earlier annotated flow

Decision 03 · Trial comparison

Put important differences side by side.

Design rationale

Trial choices contain many details. A side-by-side view makes those differences visible together rather than relying on memory.

My contribution

I worked on trial comparison within the guided-journey concept. This is a design response to information complexity; a measured improvement in patient comprehension was not established.

Decision 04 · Caregivers, privacy & consent

Let patients choose who participates—and what they can see.

What we learned

Round 1 raised unwanted community messaging. Later feedback focused on privacy and caregiver data sharing.

My response

I proposed approval-based peer connection requests and worked on Care Circle interactions and sharing controls. The team refined patient control over caregiver participation.

Explore peer connections and Care Circle artifacts

Designing for understanding and control

Accessibility considerations

Focused steps, aligned comparison categories, and explanations of medical language were design responses to cognitive effort. Patient accessibility testing and formal conformance results are not documented.

Privacy & consent considerations

Manual entry, caregiver permissions, and approved peer connections express patient choice. These prototype controls do not establish implemented security, regulatory compliance, or an operational consent system.

Explore education and the assistant concept

I led educational resources, FAQs, and the educational assistant interface. Separately, I used AI in my own process to accelerate low- and high-fidelity prototypes and communicate concepts with designers and non-designers.

Challenge

Clinical trial discovery asked patients to navigate complexity at an already difficult moment.

Patients needed to make sense of complex medical information, fragmented trial details, caregiver involvement, and real logistical constraints without the interface implying more certainty than the system could support.

What shaped the direction

Three constraints shaped the product direction.

01

Medical language needed support.

Terminology created comprehension barriers, so the experience needed to explain without oversimplifying healthcare information.

02

Caregivers needed a defined role.

Caregivers were important to patient support, but the design needed to respect patient control and information-sharing boundaries.

03

Fit was more than eligibility.

Travel and scheduling constraints could make a trial impractical even when the medical match appeared relevant.

See research foundation

The team used literature review, affinity mapping, recurring physician collaboration, and three rounds of physician think-aloud testing. Real patients were not tested because of healthcare constraints.

Key decisions

Design decisions connected physician feedback to product changes.

Decision 01

Reduce manual onboarding effort without removing user control.

Challenge: Earlier concepts included detailed manual medical history entry and document upload, which added effort and complexity.

Team thinking: The team moved toward an EHR-sync option while keeping manual entry available for patients who preferred not to share records.

My contribution: I owned key parts of the onboarding flow and translated physician feedback into refinements.

Decision 02

Help patients compare options without relying on memory.

Challenge: Trial options involved many details that could be difficult to hold in mind.

My thinking: Side-by-side comparison made important differences directly visible and reduced mental effort during selection.

Final decision: Trial comparison became a focused part of the guided-journey concept I worked on.

Decision 03

Give patients more control over social and caregiver interactions.

Peer connections

Physicians raised concerns about unwanted or excessive messaging. I proposed a connection-request model so patients had to approve peer communication.

Caregiver control

Later physician feedback emphasized privacy and caregiver data sharing. The team responded with clearer patient control over caregiver participation.

Decision 04

Use language that reflects what the system actually does.

Physician feedback showed that "Matching" implied more certainty than the system could support. The team changed the label to "Suggested Trials."
